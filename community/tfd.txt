Have you noticed how often we worry about things that haven’t even happened?

Our daughter is late returning from office. We know she may be held up with work. But as the minutes pass, our mind starts imagining all kinds of things. We call her, but she doesn’t answer. We don’t want to disturb her by calling again. Yet, how do we stop worrying?

We develop a slight sore throat. Immediately, we wonder whether it could be something serious.

We read a WhatsApp message about someone’s sudden illness or death. For the rest of the day, we keep thinking about our own health and family.

Our son and daughter-in-law haven’t called for a few days. We understand that they have their own lives. Still, a small thought hurts us. Have we become less important to them?

And then come thoughts about old age. Will we remain healthy? Will our children be around when we need them? Will we become dependent on others?

Sometimes, nothing has happened. Nobody has hurt us. No bad news has come. Yet, we have already spent hours feeling anxious and unhappy.

Why do we do this to ourselves?

An incident from the Mahabharata

Just before the Kurukshetra war, Arjuna stood on the battlefield with Krishna as his charioteer.

When he looked at the people standing on the opposite side, he saw his own teachers, elders, cousins and relatives.

His mind began to race. What if I have to kill them? What will happen to their families? What is the use of winning a kingdom at such a cost?

He began imagining the terrible consequences of the war even before it had begun.

His hands trembled. His mouth became dry. He could not even hold his bow. He told Krishna that he did not want to fight.

Krishna listened to him and guided him through the Bhagavad Gita.

He helped Arjuna see things clearly and understand his duty. He brought him back to the situation before him.

Something to reflect upon

Are we not sometimes like Arjuna?

Our daughter is ten minutes late, and our mind has already imagined an accident.

We have a small health problem, and our mind has already imagined a serious illness.

Our children don’t call for a few days, and we have already imagined a lonely old age.

Of course, our concerns are genuine. We love our families. We care about our health. We want to feel secure.

But are we helping ourselves or hurting ourselves by repeatedly imagining the worst?

We cannot control everything that happens in life. But we can certainly try not to suffer over things that haven’t happened.

A small takeaway: Three questions

The next time a worrying thought troubles us, let us pause and ask:

1. What is the fact? What do I really know?

2. What is my fear? Am I imagining something that hasn’t happened?

3. What can I do now? Is there one sensible action I can take?

If our daughter is late, we can call her or send a message. If we have a health concern, we can consult a doctor. If our children haven’t called, we can call them.

Let us do what is needed. After that, let us try not to keep replaying the same thoughts.

It may not be easy. But perhaps we can begin with just one worry at a time.

A thought to carry with us

I sometimes wonder how much of our happiness is lost, not because of what life has done to us, but because of what we imagine life might do to us.

We cannot fight every possible battle in our minds. Let us not borrow tomorrow’s sorrow and bring it into today.

The next time we catch ourselves worrying, perhaps we can ask:

Has this really happened, or am I making myself suffer over something that may never happen?

How many Kurukshetras do we create in our minds every day, even before the battle has begun?

Don’t fight tomorrow’s battle today. Let us take care of today. Tomorrow, we will face what comes.
